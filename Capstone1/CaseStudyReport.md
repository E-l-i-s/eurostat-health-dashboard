# Case Study Selection Report

## Policy Context

Physical inactivity is the fourth leading risk factor for global mortality, responsible for an estimated 3.2 million deaths annually according to the World Health Organization. Within the European Union, the Eurostat European Health Interview Survey (EHIS) provides the most comprehensive standardized framework for monitoring health determinants and outcomes across member states. I selected EHIS waves 2014 and 2019 as the data foundation because they represent two complete survey rounds with consistent methodology, enabling cross-temporal analysis of physical activity patterns and chronic disease prevalence across 35 European countries.

The WHO 2020 guidelines recommend adults aged 18-64 engage in at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity per week, or 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity, or an equivalent combination. Despite this standard, Eurostat reports indicate that a substantial proportion of the European population falls short of these targets, with significant variation by country, age group, and sex. This variation, and its relationship to chronic disease outcomes, forms the analytical core of this project.

## Case Study A: Physical Activity Insufficiency Across Europe

Case Study A asks: **How do rates of physical activity insufficiency vary by country, age group, and sex across European countries, and which demographic segments face the greatest risk of falling below WHO guidelines?**

Country-level analysis of physical activity rates matters because policy interventions are designed and funded at the national level. Identifying countries where inactivity exceeds 50% in specific demographic groups enables targeted public health campaigns rather than blanket approaches. The demographic breakdowns by age (eight brackets from 18-24 through 75+) and sex (male, female, total) are analytically useful because physical activity patterns change across the life course and differ systematically between men and women in most European countries. Understanding these gradients informs whether interventions should be age-specific, sex-specific, or both.

## Case Study B: Chronic Disease Prevalence Patterns

Case Study B asks: **Which chronic conditions — obesity, diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease — show the highest prevalence across European countries, and do the geographic patterns of chronic disease burden align with measured physical activity deficits?**

I selected four chronic condition categories from the EHIS self-reported module: obesity (BMI >= 30 based on self-reported height and weight), diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease (including coronary heart disease and stroke). These conditions represent the primary non-communicable disease burden in Europe and share physical inactivity as a common modifiable risk factor. Including multiple conditions allows for comparison of which diseases show the strongest geographic correlation with inactivity and whether the relationship is consistent across conditions.

## Methodological Note on Self-Reported Survey Data

The EHIS relies on self-reported health information rather than clinical measurements. This approach offers substantial strengths: standardized questionnaires administered across all EU member states produce comparable data for approximately 35 countries, covering demographic subgroups at a granularity that clinical registries rarely achieve. The sample sizes (approximately 150,000-200,000 respondents per wave) support reliable estimation even for small countries and narrow demographic bands.

The limitation is self-report bias. Respondents systematically overreport physical activity (social desirability bias) and underreport weight while overreporting height (leading to underestimation of BMI-based obesity prevalence). Chronic condition reporting depends on diagnosis awareness, which varies with healthcare access and screening rates across countries. These biases are consistent within waves but may vary between countries, meaning cross-country comparisons should be interpreted as rankings of self-reported health rather than clinical measurements.

## Research Questions

1. What is the relationship between physical activity insufficiency rates and chronic disease prevalence across European countries, and does this relationship hold across demographic subgroups defined by age and sex?

2. Which European countries face the greatest combined burden of high physical inactivity and high chronic disease prevalence, and has this burden increased or decreased between the 2014 and 2019 EHIS survey waves?
